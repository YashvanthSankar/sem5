How many groups do you see?

This is the same kind of data as the SVM lecture, but with the pass/fail column removed. Hover over a dot to see the student. Ask the class how many groups they see; click a button to record a guess. When someone says they see three groups, what they really mean is that there are at least three reference points, and the other students sit close to one of them. Those reference points are the centres that k-means looks for, and the number of them is k, which we must supply before the algorithm starts. We will return to how to choose k near the end.

Pick three centres

We want k = 3 groups. Each group will have a centre, the ✕ markers. Drag them anywhere.

Rule 1. Every student joins the nearest centre.

Where would you put the centres so that every student is close to one?

A centre is just a point in the plot. Drag the three crosses and watch what happens: every student takes the colour and shape of the nearest centre. Switch on territories to see the region each centre owns. This is Rule 1 of k-means: a student belongs to its nearest centre. Ask: where would you put the centres?

How good are the centres?

A good centre is close to its students. So add up how far away they are.

SSE = Σ (distance to own centre)²

Sum of squared errors. Squaring makes far-away students count most.

Make the SSE as small as you can.

We need a score for a set of centres. Measure, for each student, the distance to its own centre, square it, and add them all up. That is the SSE, the sum of squared errors. Click show distances to see the segments being measured. Challenge: drag the centres to get the SSE as low as you can; the number beneath is the best that k-means itself finds, so you know the target. Why squared? It punishes far-away students and, as the next slide shows, it gives a very simple best centre.

The best centre is the mean

One cluster of five students. Their hours studied: 2, 3, 5, 8, 12.

Drag the centre c. Each square’s area is one squared distance.

c = 9.0
SSE = 0

d/dc Σ(xᵢ − c)² = −2 Σ(xᵢ − c) = 0

Rule 2. The best centre is the mean.

Rule 1 told us how to assign students to centres. Rule 2 tells us where a centre should go. Take one cluster, five numbers, and drag the centre. Each coloured square has side equal to the distance from a student to the centre, so its area is the squared distance; the total area is the SSE. The curve underneath shows SSE against c: it is a parabola with its minimum exactly at the mean, six. Calculus agrees: differentiate the sum of squares, set it to zero, and the solution is the average of the numbers. So Rule 2: move each centre to the mean of its students.

Two steps, repeated

0 · Choose k starting centres.

1 · Assign. Every student joins the nearest centre. (Rule 1)

2 · Update. Every centre moves to the mean of its students. (Rule 2)

3 · Repeat 1 and 2 until no student changes cluster.

Put the two rules together and you have the whole algorithm, called Lloyd’s algorithm. Start with k centres, anywhere. Assign every student to the nearest centre. Move every centre to the mean of its students. Because the centres moved, some students may now be nearer a different centre, so repeat. Stop when an assign step changes nobody. That is it. By hand: six students, two centres

The same algorithm with numbers you can check on paper. Six students; the starting centres are the first two students. Iteration 1: compute each student’s distance to both centres (Pythagoras), pick the smaller. Then each new centre is the average of its students. Iteration 2 repeats with the new centres; the student at (2, 1) now prefers the left centre. Iteration 3: nobody changes, so we stop. Press Next to step through; the table and the plot show the same thing.

Does it always stop?

Assign moves a student only to a nearer centre, so the SSE cannot go up.

Update moves a centre to the mean, the best spot for its students, so the SSE cannot go up.

There are only finitely many ways to split 24 students into groups, and the SSE never repeats. So the loop must stop.

Two short arguments. One: assigning each student to its nearest centre can only reduce the total squared distance. Two: moving a centre to the mean of its students can only reduce it, because the mean is the minimiser. So SSE goes down every step, and since there are finitely many ways to split the students, it must stop. Click to reveal the chart and press the button: six random starts, six curves, every one of them non-increasing. But look at where they end: not all at the same height. That is the next slide.

Different starts, different answers

Run k-means from a random start. Hollow rings: where the centres began.

A local minimum: not always the best.

Fix: run several starts, keep the lowest SSE.

Press Another random start several times. Sometimes k-means finds three sensible groups. Sometimes it ends with two centres sharing one real group and one centre stretched across two others, and it stays there, because from that position neither rule can improve things. SSE is lower for the good answers. The algorithm always stops, but only at a local minimum. The usual remedy is simple: run it several times from different starts and keep the answer with the lowest SSE. Run 20 starts shows how often each answer appears.

Better starts: k-means++

Choose starting centres that are far apart.

First centre: a random student. Each next centre: a student chosen with probability proportional to (distance to the nearest centre so far)². Big dots = likely.

Random starts fail because two centres can begin in the same group. k-means++ makes that unlikely: pick the first centre at random, then pick each next centre with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest centre already chosen, so far-away students are likely and nearby ones are not. Press Add a centre and watch the dot sizes. Then compare: 300 runs from random students against 300 runs from k-means++. The bars show how often each reaches the best SSE. scikit-learn uses k-means++ by default.

How many clusters?

k-means needs k before it starts. Slide it.

k = 3

SSE with the best of 20 starts

0

More clusters always means a smaller SSE.

At k = 24 every student is its own cluster and SSE = 0. Useless. We need a better rule than “smallest SSE”.

Drag k from 1 to 8. For each k the picture shows the best clustering from twenty k-means++ starts. The SSE keeps falling as k grows, because more centres always sit closer to students. Taken to the extreme, k equal to 24 gives every student a private cluster and SSE zero. So the smallest SSE cannot choose k. We look instead for the point where adding a cluster stops paying off.

Look for the elbow

k = 3

Elbow: stop where one more cluster stops paying off. Here: k = 3.

Silhouette: how much closer a student is to its own cluster than to the next one (−1 to 1). Pick the peak.

The first chart is SSE against k. It drops steeply from one to three clusters, then flattens: that bend is the elbow, and it suggests k equals three. The second chart is the average silhouette: for each student, compare its average distance to its own cluster with its average distance to the nearest other cluster; one means a perfect fit, zero means on the border, negative means probably in the wrong cluster. It peaks at k equal to three too. Real data is rarely this clean, so treat both as hints, and ask whether the clusters make sense for the problem.

Six students, raw units

colour = cluster · shape = real group: ● few hours, ■ many hours

Six students, k = 2, and the same starting centres as before: the first two students. We use raw units, so one hour counts the same as one mark. Look at each squared distance, split into its hours part and its score part. The score part is hundreds; the hours part is tens at most, so the score decides which centre is nearer. Step through: assign, update, assign, update, assign. The two final centres have almost the same hours (5.5 and 5.25): the clusters differ only in score. But the real groups, shown by the shapes, are defined by hours, and the colours do not match them.

The same six, standardised

colour = cluster · shape = real group: ● few hours, ■ many hours

Now the same six students and the same starting students, but first standardise each feature: subtract its mean and divide by its standard deviation. Hours and score now both have mean zero and standard deviation one, so the two parts of each squared distance are of comparable size: hours supply around a third of the total instead of three percent. Step through assign and update again. This time the assignment follows hours, the final clusters are exactly the real groups, and the two centres sit far apart in hours.

Same students, two answers

Same students, same algorithm. Only the units changed.

The two final answers on the same picture. Shading shows the territory of each centre. In raw units the territories are separated by a horizontal line: the score alone decides, and the clusters mix the two real groups. After standardising, the dividing line is nearly vertical and the clusters are exactly the real groups. The data and the algorithm are identical; only the units differ. So standardise whenever features are on different scales.

One outlier drags the mean

One cluster of nine students and one unusual student. Drag the red one.

The mean (✕) follows the outlier. The median (◇) hardly moves.

k-means centres are means, so outliers pull them. Remove outliers first, or use k-medoids.

A cluster of nine ordinary students and one outlier. Drag the red student far away: the mean, which is what k-means uses as the centre, follows it, and the cluster’s centre ends up far from the nine students it is supposed to describe. The median, shown as a hollow diamond, barely moves. This is why outliers hurt k-means, and why variants such as k-medoids exist, which use an actual student as the centre.

k-means likes round clusters

k-means draws straight borders between round blobs of similar size. For other shapes, try DBSCAN or Gaussian mixtures.

Every centre owns the region closest to it, so the borders between clusters are straight lines. That works when clusters are round blobs of similar spread. Try the other data sets: two interleaved moons, two concentric rings and two long bars are all cut in the wrong place, even with the best of ten starts. Switch on the real groups: colours are what k-means found, shapes are the truth. When your clusters are not blob-shaped, use a different method, such as DBSCAN or a Gaussian mixture.

Compress an image to k colours

original: 16.7 million possible colours

k = 8 colours

Each pixel is a point in (red, green, blue) space. The k centres become the palette, and every pixel takes the colour of its nearest centre.

A real use of k-means. Every pixel of this picture is a point in three-dimensional colour space: its red, green and blue values. Run k-means on all the pixels with k colours; the centres are the palette, and every pixel is replaced by its nearest centre. At k equal to eight the picture still looks like the original but needs only three bits per pixel instead of twenty-four. The same idea is used for image compression, and for finding the dominant colours of a photo.

In scikit-learn

from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

X = df[["hours", "score"]]
model = make_pipeline(
    StandardScaler(),                   # one scale
    KMeans(n_clusters=3, n_init=10,
           random_state=0))
labels = model.fit_predict(X)

km = model[-1]
km.cluster_centers_   # the k centres
km.inertia_           # the SSE

n_clusters is k. You choose it.

n_init random starts; the lowest inertia_ wins. k-means++ is the default.

StandardScaler first, because distances need comparable units.

inertia_ is exactly the SSE we met today.

In practice you do not write the loop yourself. KMeans takes n_clusters, which is k, and n_init, the number of random starts; it keeps the best one, measured by inertia, which is exactly our SSE. Put a StandardScaler in front in a pipeline, so that distances are computed on comparable scales. The fitted model gives you cluster_centers_, labels_ and inertia_.

What to remember

1Two steps

Assign every student to the nearest centre. Move every centre to the mean of its students.

2One score

SSE, the sum of squared distances to the centres. Both steps lower it, so the loop stops.

3Starts matter

It stops at a local minimum. Use k-means++ and several starts (n_init).

4Choose k

Not by the smallest SSE. Look for the elbow and the silhouette peak.

5Scale first

Distances need comparable units. Standardise the features.

6Know the limits

Round blobs of similar size, and means are pulled by outliers.

Six ideas. One: the two steps. Two: the SSE that both steps lower. Three: local minima and how to fight them. Four: choosing k with the elbow and silhouette. Five: standardise the features. Six: k-means assumes round, similar-sized clusters and is sensitive to outliers.
